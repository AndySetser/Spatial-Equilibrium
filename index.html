This tool doesn't rank or score places. It shows what the data actually says, with the source and definition next to every number, so you can decide what matters to you.

Why the same word can mean different things

The Census Bureau and the Bureau of Economic Analysis both use the word "income," but they don't mean the same thing. ACS median household income comes from a survey. It's pre-tax cash: wages, self-employment income, and some cash payments like Social Security. It doesn't count employer-paid benefits, non-cash aid, or capital gains. BEA per-capita personal income is built differently, from business and administrative records. It's broader: it adds in things like employer-paid health insurance, the value of owning your home instead of renting it, and government benefits like Medicare. Same word, two different measurements. Treat them as two separate views of a place, not one number you can swap for the other.

How the rent burden number is built

A quick way to check if rent is affordable is to compare it to everyone's income, owners included. But that mixes two different groups and makes rent look more affordable than it really is for renters. This tool compares rent only to what renters themselves earn (Census Table B25119), so the number reflects the people actually paying rent. It's still a simplified estimate built from two separate medians, not a household-by-household calculation. The Census Bureau does have tables that measure that directly (B25070 and B25091), but they aren't available at every geography level this tool covers.

Percentiles, standard deviations, and the regression

Any percentile or standard-deviation figure comes from comparing this place against every U.S. county, or every place in City/Town mode, pulled the same way. Percentile means rank: 80th percentile means this place is higher than 80 percent of the others. Standard deviation just means how far from the average, in a consistent unit.

In County mode, the "home value vs. predicted" number comes from a hedonic regression: a statistical fit of home value against income and the natural amenity score together, run across every county with data for both. It shows what home values would be expected to look like at this county's income and amenity level, based on the national pattern, and how far the real number is from that. When the amenity score is missing (mostly Alaska and Hawaii, which aren't in the USDA dataset), the tool falls back to a simpler income-only version and says so. Either way, it's a small model. It leaves out zoning rules, school quality, crime, and plenty of other things that actually move home prices, so read the result as a rough national pattern, not a formula for any one place. If the national dataset is still loading in the background, this number is just left off rather than guessed.

What this tool doesn't do well yet

ACS 5-year estimates run 2 to 7 years behind real time, and small places have wider margins of error than large ones, even though this tool shows every number with the same visual weight. City/Town mode only covers incorporated places and Census-designated places. Small unincorporated communities won't show up. When a number is missing or suppressed for privacy, it says "data not available" instead of guessing. Wage and industry detail, migration patterns, climate risk, and broadband access aren't in here yet, but they're on the list.
