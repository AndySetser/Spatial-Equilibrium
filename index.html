This tool does not rank, score, or recommend any place over another. It shows what federal data says, with each figure's source, exact definition, and known limitations attached, so you can judge what it means for your own decision.

Why the same word can mean different things

"Income" from the Census Bureau and "income" from the Bureau of Economic Analysis are not the same measurement, even though both use that word. ACS median household income is pre-tax cash income reported by households in a survey — wages, self-employment income, some cash transfers — and excludes employer-paid benefits, non-cash government aid, and capital gains. BEA per-capita personal income is a broader national-accounts concept built from administrative and business records: it includes employer contributions to health insurance and pensions, imputed rent on owner-occupied housing, and the value of in-kind government transfers like Medicare. The two numbers are constructed from different source data and different definitions of "income" — treat them as two different lenses on the same place, never as directly interchangeable, and never subtract one from the other.

How the rent burden figure is built (and why it differs from a naive version)

A common shortcut compares median rent against the income of all households — owners included — which mixes two different populations. This tool instead compares median gross rent against the median income of renter households specifically (Census Table B25119), so the burden estimate reflects what renters actually earn, not a blended figure inflated by higher-earning homeowners. It is still a simplified estimate built from two separate medians, not a household-by-household ratio — the Census Bureau's own housing-cost-burden tables (B25070/B25091) measure that directly but aren't broken out at every geography level this tool covers.

Statistical context: percentiles, z-scores, and the hedonic regression

Where you see a percentile or standard-deviation figure, it's computed against a live national dataset of every U.S. county (or every place, in City/Town mode) pulled the same way, in the same batched fetch used to build the search list. The percentile is this place's rank among all of them; the z-score is how many standard deviations it sits from the national mean.

In County mode, the "home value vs. predicted" figure uses a hedonic regression — home value regressed on income, amenity business density, and the natural amenity score together — whenever all three are available for that county, so the predicted value and each coefficient reflect that variable's relationship to home price holding the other two constant. When amenity data is missing for a place, this tool falls back to a simpler income-only regression and says so explicitly rather than silently degrading precision. Either version is a small model: it leaves out land-use restrictions, school quality, crime, coastal proximity, and dozens of other real drivers of home prices, so the coefficients and residuals shown are rough national averages, not a causal formula for any one place. If the national dataset hasn't finished loading (a one-time background pull), these figures are simply omitted rather than estimated.

Known limits, stated plainly

ACS 5-year estimates lag real time by roughly 2–7 years and carry wider margins of error in small places — a number for a town of 800 people is far less statistically reliable than the same figure for a city of 80,000, even though this tool displays them with equal visual weight. City/Town mode covers incorporated places and Census-designated places only; unincorporated communities without their own Census geography won't appear. Suppressed or unavailable figures are labeled "data not available," never estimated or guessed. BLS wage and industry detail, migration flows, climate risk, and broadband access are planned additions, not yet included.
